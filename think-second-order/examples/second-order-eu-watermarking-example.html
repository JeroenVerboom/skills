Second-order analysis

The EU now requires AI-generated content to be watermarked. And then what?

The obvious first-order effect is that audiences can finally tell AI content from human content. This traces the move past that first step, along three threads: the detection race, the market it reshapes, and the trust it can build.

And then what EU AI Act, Article 50, content marking, applicable 2 August 2026

Second-order thinking traces a decision past its immediate effect by asking "and then what" repeatedly, following how other people respond. This move has more than one second-order thread, so we trace three: use the tabs below to follow each. The consequences are directions of travel, not predictions.

The call: Modify

Comply as accountability, not as a fix for deception.

Split your Article 50 work into the marking you can enforce and the marking you cannot, before 2 August 2026

All three threads point the same way. The enforceable value is the visible "this is AI" disclosure, verifiable provenance on high-stakes content, and a compliant vendor. The wasted effort is treating invisible text watermarks as a way to stop misuse, because they come off under paraphrase. The durable upside, provenance as a trust signal, is worth building for on its own terms.

The consequence chains

One move, three second-order threads. Each follows a different set of people, and each carries its own feedback loop and its own 10/10/10 read.

Follow what happens to the mark itself, as providers, platforms and bad actors each respond.

  1. 1st
    order

    Providers and deployers

    Everything gets marked and labelled

    Providers add machine-readable marks and invisible watermarks to their output; deployers add a visible "this is AI" line. On its face, any reader can now separate AI content from human content.

  2. 2nd
    order

    Platforms and bad actors

    The label falls off exactly where it is needed

    Every major platform strips provenance data (a signed record of where a file came from) on upload, and a screenshot erases it. Anyone set on deceiving paraphrases the AI text, which defeats the watermark. Honest content stays labelled; deceptive content routes around the label.

  3. 3rd
    order

    Audiences and the market

    The signal inverts and the label loses its meaning

    A detection race opens, and "unmarked" starts to read as "human and trustworthy", so the reward for removing marks grows. Compliant organisations carry the labels; the actors the rule meant to catch evade them.

Feedback loop

Reinforcing

The marking arms race

Every improvement in marking invites a matching improvement in stripping or paraphrasing. Better detectors are used to train better evaders, and the outputs of each side become the training data for the other. The race never settles.

The 10/10/10 read

In 10 minutes

Relief

"We added a disclosure line and picked a compliant vendor, so we are covered." The box is ticked and the worry lifts.

In 10 months

The label sits on the wrong content

It is on all your compliant output and absent on the content that actually matters, so the argument about text watermarking reopens.

In 10 years

Stripping is a known routine

The mark is treated as removable, so nobody relies on its absence to prove anything. The durable value was the visible disclosure.

Follow the money and the market structure, as compliance teams, vendors and smaller builders respond.

  1. 1st
    order

    Compliance teams

    Every team that touches generative AI takes on new work

    Before 2 August 2026, each team inventories where it makes synthetic content, adds the disclosures, and documents its human editorial review. A real but largely one-off cost.

  2. 2nd
    order

    AI vendors and buyers

    Marking gets bundled and priced in

    Vendors build compliant marking into their platforms and charge for it. Buyers prefer the few large providers that can prove they follow the voluntary Code of Practice (the safe-harbour guidance), because that is the low-risk path.

  3. 3rd
    order

    Smaller builders and the market

    Compliance becomes a moat

    Robust marking is easy for large providers and hard for small teams and open-source tools. The cost of proving compliance squeezes smaller EU builders, and the market quietly concentrates around a handful of vendors.

Feedback loops

Reinforcing

Compliance favours scale

The more marking standardises around big vendors, the more compliance rewards buying from them, which sends more buyers their way and standardises the market further.

Balancing

Open provenance lowers the floor

Content Credentials are an open standard, not owned by one vendor, so over time cheaper and open tools can emit the same marks, which eases the squeeze on smaller builders.

The 10/10/10 read

In 10 minutes

Buy the problem away

"We will just pick a compliant vendor and move on." The cost reads as a one-off purchase.

In 10 months

Compliance is a procurement line

It has become a budget item and a vendor-selection criterion, and small suppliers start to fall off the shortlist.

In 10 years

Marking is a commodity feature

Bundled everywhere and cheap, but the market concentrated during the transition, and that concentration is hard to reverse.

Follow the upside, as brands, audiences and the wider market respond to content that can prove its origin.

  1. 1st
    order

    Brands and publishers

    Labels and provenance appear on legitimate content

    Every serious publisher and brand starts signing and labelling what it makes, first to comply, then as routine practice.

  2. 2nd
    order

    Audiences

    People stop noticing the label, but start expecting the proof

    Everyday labels fade into the background. Yet for high-stakes content, an official notice, a legal document, a news photo, audiences begin to expect verifiable provenance and treat its absence as a reason to doubt.

  3. 3rd
    order

    The market

    Provenance becomes a premium trust signal

    "Signed by a named organisation" turns into a competitive edge for journalism, legal work and official communication. Brands that adopt provenance well stand out, and it shifts from a chore to a differentiator.

Feedback loop

Balancing

Provenance where it counts

For high-stakes institutional content, verifiable provenance plus a named accountable human holds, because the receiving side has a reason to check the signal and keep it. This is the part of the duty that durably works.

The 10/10/10 read

In 10 minutes

Just another chore

The rule reads as a compliance task and nothing more.

In 10 months

Some brands market their provenance

Early adopters start using "content you can verify" as a selling point, ahead of the crowd.

In 10 years

Provenance-by-default is a trust standard

For institutional content, being able to prove origin is expected, and the rule's lasting value turns out to be here, not in universal text watermarking.

The scaling test

What if everyone marks

If all legitimate content is marked and marks are removable, the absence of a mark becomes the thing a bad actor wants. Marking then identifies the honest actors more than it catches the dishonest ones. It is still worth doing for accountability and consumer clarity, but not as a control against disinformation.

Traps in these chains

  • Mistaking the machine mark for the binding duty. The enforceable, novel obligation is the human-perceivable disclosure, the visible "this is AI" line, not the fragile watermark. Chasing the watermark spends effort where the law does not actually bind you.
  • Reading only one chain. The three threads pull in different directions: a risk (A), a market shift (B) and an upside (C). A plan that answers only the arms-race chain misses the concentration risk and the trust opportunity.
  • Only tracing the downside. Success has second-order effects too. Chain C is the reminder: honest provenance can become a real consumer-trust signal, and a differentiator for brands that adopt it well.

This is a second-order analysis: a structured trace of plausible downstream effects, not a forecast. The chains are argued, not measured, and should be pressure-tested against your own situation.
